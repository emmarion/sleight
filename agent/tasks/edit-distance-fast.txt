Your task is to write a fast function in Sleight that computes the edit
distance between two strings, certified correct against the goal in
edit-distance-fast/spec.sl. Or: to lay the groundwork for a future session to
do so. This task will likely take many sessions, so leaving
solid, well-recorded progress matters more than finishing in this one.

The distance is the one GCC uses for its spelling suggestions: an insertion or
a deletion costs 2, a substitution costs 0 between equal bytes, 1 between bytes
that differ only in ASCII case, and 2 otherwise, and swapping two adjacent
bytes costs 2. A swapped pair is not edited further (the "optimal string
alignment" distance). spec.sl defines it as `Dist xs ys d`, by its usual
recurrence. Your `edit-distance-fast` takes each string as a `Text`, its bytes
with an erased proof that their length is below `max-array-length`, and
returns the distance with its `Dist` proof.

To check your work, call `check` with std/nat.sl, std/list.sl, std/macro.sl,
std/tactics.sl, edit-distance-fast/spec.sl, then your files, one of them
proving the goal with `(qed e)`. To time it, compile the same files followed by
edit-distance-fast/perf.sl with `sl`; perf.sl calls your `edit-distance-fast`.
The finished solution must compile with no warnings, and none of your files may
contain a `;! suppress`; intermediate work may have both. A solution is not
finished while it makes more than a constant number of allocations per call,
any array write could be an `-imm` one, any runtime arithmetic could be a
`u62-*` call, or any runtime recursion could be a tail call, even if
measurements show no gain.

For reference: MANUAL.md (the implementations and their command lines),
WARNINGS.md (the warnings the compilers emit), DESIGN.md (the language),
prelude.sl (the standard library), std/ (source libraries, among them
std/nat.sl, which holds `Le`, and std/list.sl, lemmas about `Length`, `Nth`,
`SetNth` and `Repeat`), tests/ (example programs), flat/ (a fast heap sort
certified against heapsort-fast/spec.sl; flat/README.md describes it), and
self/ (the compiler, itself written in Sleight).

Only work/, HANDOFF.md, and FEEDBACK.md carry over to the next session.
Generally put experiments and `sl` captures in scratch/, which does not carry
over; helper programs worth keeping can go in work/. Keep work/ tidy, pruning
files that have served their purpose.

Your session has two phases: first experimenting, then writing HANDOFF.md and
FEEDBACK.md. HANDOFF.md tells the next session where things stand and what to
try next; rewrite it each session instead of adding to it. FEEDBACK.md tells
Sleight's maintainers what slowed you down because of Sleight, its library, or its tools,
rather than because of the problem itself; add to it each session instead of
rewriting it. Each phase has its own budget of tokens. In the second phase,
those two are the only files you can edit; there's no need to edit them, or to
read FEEDBACK.md, before then, so spend the first phase on the task itself.

Hint: perf.sl times the call alone and reports what it allocates. To try other
lengths, copy it to scratch/ and change both `perf-n` or `perf-m` and the
numeral that its `-fits` bound names.

Hint: to compile without running, use the `sl` tool's `ocaml` option.

Hint: a byte read with `bytes-get-many` is a `U8`, whose code a `let-match` on
`MkU8` gives, and a `let-match` on `MkText` unwraps a `Text` at no cost, since
it is a newtype.

Hint: the costs 0, 1 and 2 in spec.sl are numerals, opaque to the checker;
`(unary 2)` and its like give the rules that `Add` proofs over them need, and
`prove-literal-lt` proves a bound between numerals in one line.
